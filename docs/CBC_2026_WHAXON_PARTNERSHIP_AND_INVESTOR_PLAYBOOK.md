## 1. Executive objective

WHAXON is the most mature project in the portfolio and the only one that should be presented as the immediate commercial product. The other projects (KILN, Aletheia, SYBIL and ZALMOXIS) are longer-term research/product directions, not integrated WHAXON capabilities. Do not imply otherwise.

The objective at CBC is not to collect business cards or pitch every exhibitor. It is to leave with:

1. **One or more qualified pilot conversations** with a cybersecurity services provider or end-user organization.
2. **A credible strategic partner lead** able to provide engineering, deployment infrastructure, distribution, customer access or operational expertise.
3. **A financing pathway** appropriate to the project's current stage and the founder's current legal/company status.
4. **Specific follow-up commitments**: named owner, next meeting, requested material and date.

A partner who provides a first deployment, infrastructure or customer access may be more valuable immediately than an investor who cannot invest at the current stage.

### The core positioning

> WHAXON is an operator-governed cybersecurity assessment and validation platform. It orchestrates supported security tools, applies explicit scope controls, normalizes findings, validates selected finding types deterministically, preserves evidence and produces engagement reports. It is being developed for authorized security assessments, with the CLI as the dependable operator interface and a browser UI as the operational/presentation layer.

Keep the product claim aligned with the actual repository and current acceptance evidence. WHAXON is not a universal autonomous pentester, does not guarantee complete vulnerability coverage, and is not a hosted scanning service.

### The ask

Do not open with “I need money.” Open with the product, the problem and the reason the other party is relevant. Then ask for one specific next step:

- a controlled pilot;
- a technical/product review;
- a channel or delivery partnership discussion;
- infrastructure or engineering support;
- an introduction to the relevant decision-maker;
- advice on an eligible financing route.

### Strategic segmentation refinement

The original priority list remains the working target list. Within that list:

1. **First commercial wedge:** Toulouse/Occitanie MSSPs, boutique pentest consultancies and technical service-delivery owners with a repeatable assessment, validation, triage, evidence or report-production problem. **Trust-layer language:** The brief proposes a pitch centered on a fail-closed SQLite DAG and a `candidate → verified/inconclusive` triage state machine. Never say WHAXON *eliminates hallucinations*, *eliminates scanner false positives*, or validates *all* findings. A safer buyer statement is: “WHAXON aims to help operators distinguish supported, evidence-backed validation outcomes from unresolved candidates while preserving a reproducible audit trail.” Use exact implementation terminology only when the live demonstrated engine version supports it.

**Why prioritize:** Advens provides cybersecurity consulting and services, including SOC, vulnerability/exposure management, incident response and security consulting. **Opening (in person or email):**

> Hello Mr. Francillon. I'm an independent developer based in the Toulouse area, building WHAXON, an operator-governed platform for authorized security assessments. It orchestrates supported tools, consolidates findings, validates selected findings with deterministic checks and preserves evidence for reporting. I saw that your Occitanie team will be at CBC on stand 15. I'd value 20 minutes to show you the working CLI workflow and understand whether it could complement an Advens consulting or assessment workflow.

**What to ask:**

1. Which recurring part of your assessment workflow is most expensive or difficult to reproduce consistently: tool orchestration, evidence collection, validation, retesting or reporting?
2. Would Advens consider a tightly scoped pilot on a lab or explicitly authorized client environment?
3. What would WHAXON have to demonstrate before your team would trust it in a real engagement?
4. Who owns technical evaluation and partnership decisions for the South-West team?
5. If a pilot is premature, could you introduce me to the colleague best placed to assess it?

**Specific pilot proposal:** one bounded engagement on a customer-approved lab or test environment; agree scope and rules of engagement in writing; run a defined set of supported discovery/enumeration/validation workflows; compare normalized findings, evidence completeness, analyst time and report quality against the existing process. No production testing without written authorization and explicit rules.

**What to show:** a short, real CLI workflow from engagement/scope setup through supported tool execution, findings, deterministic validation, evidence and report. Show an actual output generated by the current code, not slides or mocked data. Be candid about supported finding types and any incomplete workflows.

**Avoid:** claiming universal pentest automation, claiming every finding is verified, claiming the UI is fully complete if it is not, or asking Advens to resell an unproven product immediately.

**Desired outcome:** a named technical evaluator and a date for a follow-up demonstration or pilot-scoping call.

## 7. The WHAXON demonstration — what to show

The demonstration must be real and repeatable. Do not rely on screenshots alone or present a planned capability as working.

### Recommended 8–10 minute sequence

1. **Position the product (45 seconds).** Explain the problem: fragmented tool execution, inconsistent findings, weak reproducibility and expensive reporting.
2. **Show the CLI (1 minute).** Start with help/version and a prepared lab engagement. Emphasize that the CLI is the operator's primary workflow and does not depend on the browser UI.
3. **Show authorization and scope (1 minute).** Demonstrate the explicit in-scope/out-of-scope configuration and explain that written authorization and external egress controls remain necessary.
4. **Run a controlled lab workflow (2 minutes).** Show discovery/enumeration and a supported scanner/tool adapter. Use only a target you own or have written permission to test.
5. **Show orchestration and findings (1 minute).** Explain how tool output becomes normalized findings and how follow-up work is handled. Do not claim every tool or follow-up path is complete unless tested.
6. **Show deterministic validation (1–2 minutes).** Demonstrate one finding type that is genuinely supported. Explain candidate/verified/inconclusive semantics. An LLM does not assign the verification verdict.
7. **Show evidence and retest (1 minute).** Show preserved request/response or other relevant evidence, provenance and the current retest workflow where supported.
8. **Show the report (1 minute).** Open a report generated from the same engagement. Point out evidence, severity, limitations and what was not tested.
9. **Show the UI only if its exposed workflows are live (optional).** Use the supplied UI and real backend. Hide or avoid pages/controls that remain unimplemented or unverified.

### Demo safety checklist

- Use a disposable local lab or a specifically authorized test target.
- Have a pre-run dataset and a backup demonstration path, but label recorded material honestly.
- Disable external network access unless required for the controlled lab.
- Do not expose credentials, tokens, customer data, private URLs or personal information.
- Have a known-good report already generated in case the live demo fails.
- Do not run scans against an exhibitor, event network, public IP or another attendee's device without explicit written authorization.
- If a capability fails, say so plainly and show the last verified state. Do not improvise a claim.
### Questions to ask the prospect during the demo

- Which part of this workflow is most valuable to your team?
- What would prevent you from using it?
- Which evidence or report fields are mandatory?
- What tool integrations are essential in your environment?
- What would a successful 2–4 week pilot have to prove?
- Who needs to approve a pilot and what would the rules of engagement require?

## 13. Conversation discipline and qualification

Use this simple sequence in every meeting:

1. **Ask:** “What are you responsible for, and what are you currently trying to improve?”
2. **Listen:** identify one real workflow pain.
3. **Connect:** explain only the WHAXON capability that addresses that pain.
4. **Demonstrate:** show the smallest real workflow that proves the point.
5. **Qualify:** ask about owner, timing, approval process, environment and success criteria.
6. **Ask:** propose one next step.
7. **Record:** write down the name, role, exact request and agreed follow-up.

### Qualify a potential partner

- Does the organization have a relevant customer or internal workflow?
- Is there a person who owns the problem?
- Can they provide a test environment or authorized pilot?
- Can they contribute technical feedback or infrastructure?
- Is there a realistic route to a paid deployment?
- What procurement, legal, security and support requirements apply?
- What is the next action and date?

### Red flags

- They want free unlimited development with no defined pilot.
- They ask for broad exclusivity before proving commercial value.
- They request access to source code or IP before an agreed evaluation framework.
- They want WHAXON to test a third-party target without written authorization.
- They ask you to make claims or sign commitments you cannot substantiate.
- They offer “exposure” but no concrete introduction, pilot or deliverable.

Do not sign an NDA, exclusivity, IP assignment, reseller agreement, investment term sheet or customer contract on the spot without reviewing it carefully and obtaining appropriate legal advice.

## §26.0 — Purpose and capability model

The add-on exists to help a single hunter find and prove the bug classes that YesWeHack-style programs pay for: IDOR, horizontal/vertical privilege escalation, authentication bypass, business logic errors with real impact, and access-control flaws in authenticated multi-user web apps.

It does this by being a program-policy-driven harness with differential analysis at its core. The hunter drives. The policy gates what the tool is allowed to do at a given target. The override log records what actually happened.

### Capability model

Capability is unconditional. Authorization is per-target and fail-closed.

The add-on contains every capability described in this document — including scanner orchestration, AI action selection, AI-authored live requests, and catalog extension. None of those capabilities are removed. All of them are gated by the active program policy. When no policy is loaded, existing WHAXON behavior applies (host-scope gate only). When a policy is loaded, the policy decides what runs.

This mirrors the existing scope.py design: the tool is capable of running nmap; scope decides whether it does. The bounty add-on extends that discipline upward to a program layer.

### The one unconditional non-goal

The add-on never weakens its own policy. No self-modification. No self-permission. No automatic expansion of the program list, the allowed tools, or the AI flags. Policy files are human-authored and human-reviewed. A malformed policy is refused, not repaired. A missing policy is refused, not defaulted-permissive.

This is R69 (authority narrows, never expands, without explicit human re-authorization) applied at the add-on level.

### Why this shape

Every program reviewed on YesWeHack (50/50 in the sampled list, including all examples) contains: "Do not use automated scanners or tools that generate large amount of network traffic." HARMAN explicitly names the legal consequence: non-compliant testing removes CFAA safe-harbor protection.

The economics: first clean report wins, systemic issues decay 100% to 10% by the sixth report, triage responds in under 1 day. The edge a solo hunter has is being correct and being first with a bug a scanner cannot find.

But the same capabilities that are prohibited on most programs are permitted on some, and on the hunter's own infrastructure, sanctioned labs, CTFs, and self-owned targets they are simply ordinary tools. The design serves both by making authorization data, not code.

## §26.1 — Program policy layer

### Purpose

Without it, the add-on refuses to touch a target. This is the outer gate. The existing scope.json remains the inner gate (host-level authorization). Both must pass.

### Location

data/programs/<slug>.yaml
data/programs/<slug>.overrides.log

The slug matches the program's URL slug (for example xelians-blackbox-program, cryptobox-bug-bounty).

### Reward model variants

Five shapes, because five platform families publish rewards differently:

| `type` | Used by | Fields |
|---|---|---|
| `grid` | YesWeHack | `grid[]` of asset_value x CVSS band -> amount |
| `severity_table` | HackerOne | `table[]` of severity -> amount |
| `vrt_tier` | Bugcrowd | `tiers[]` of P1-P5 -> points and/or amount |
| `range` | Intigriti, early programs | `min`, `max` only |
| `impact_matrix` | Immunefi | `impacts[]` of impact category x asset type -> amount |

v1 implements `grid` only (YesWeHack). The schema accepts all five so a later
platform does not force a schema rewrite. An unimplemented `type` refuses at
load time with `reward_model.type '<x>' is not implemented in v1`.

### Scope objects

Scopes are objects, not flat strings, because `asset_value` changes the payout
and `type` changes what tooling is appropriate (a mobile app scope is not
scanned by nmap). The resolver matches on `scope`; the reward calculation and
the tool-permission check both read `asset_value` and `type`.

### Platform portability (SS26.1.2)

The schema above is platform-agnostic by construction:

- Common fields are what every platform publishes.
- `platform_extras` is validated only for the named platform, passed through
  untouched for `other`.
- `reward_model` is a tagged union, so a HackerOne severity table and a
  Bugcrowd VRT tier are expressible without inventing new top-level keys.

**v1 population is `clipboard_parse`.** The human copies a program page's text
and runs `whaxon bounty policy init <slug> --from-clipboard`. The parser
(`core/bounty_parse.py`) turns the pasted text into the policy dict. No network
call to any platform. This sidesteps two real risks: YesWeHack's ToU forbids
derivative use of their site materials, and their internal API is unversioned
and changes without notice. A parser is honest and stable; an API client is
neither.

**Adding HackerOne, Bugcrowd, Intigriti, or Immunefi** is a parser per page
shape plus a `platform_extras` validator per platform. The schema does not
change. That is the portability guarantee.

### Behavior when missing or malformed

- Missing file: refuse; error names the missing file and the expected path.
- Malformed YAML: refuse; error names the parse failure and line number.
- Missing `tools.mode`: refuse. No default.
- Empty `scope.in_scope`: refuse.
- Unknown `platform`: refuse, key named.
- Unknown `reward_model.type`: refuse, type named.
- `reward_model.type` known but not implemented in v1: refuse, naming the type.
- Unknown top-level key anywhere: refuse, key named. `platform_extras` is the
  exception: its contents are validated only for the named platform.
### Relationship to existing scope.json

Both gates apply, in order: program gate (scope.in_scope in the policy), then host gate (data/scope.json). Out-of-scope wins. Existing scope.py behavior preserved.

### Choosing a policy for a target

The add-on resolves the policy by matching the target host against each policy's scope.in_scope. If exactly one matches, that policy applies. If more than one, refusal. If none, refusal for bounty subcommands.

## §26.1.1 — Override log specification

### Purpose

The override log records every moment the operator enables a capability a policy would otherwise forbid. Its purpose is to make the decision to bypass a program rule expensive, so the default is compliance and the exception is deliberate, documented, and visible at report time.

### Properties

- Append-only. Opened with O_APPEND. Never truncated, rewritten, or deleted.
- Mandatory. Writes on: enabling ai_policy.allow_action_selection, enabling allow_live_requests, enabling allow_auto_mode, running a tool from tools.denylist, running a tool outside tools.allowlist, or any manual override flag passed to a bounty subcommand.
- Human-attributed. Each entry names the invoking user and the ISO-8601 timestamp with timezone.
- Cross-checked. The report generator reads the log before producing submission-ready output.

### Entry format

One line per override, newline-delimited JSON:

{"ts":"2026-10-13T16:04:22+02:00","operator":"andreipath","slug":"example-program","kind":"tool_bypass","tool":"nmap","mode":"denylist","reason":"operator flag --allow-forbidden-tool","session_id":"job-abc123"}
{"ts":"2026-10-13T16:09:11+02:00","operator":"andreipath","slug":"example-program","kind":"ai_flag_enabled","flag":"allow_auto_mode","previous":false,"new":true,"reason":"policy edit"}

The reason field is required. No reason, no override.

### Report cross-check

When the report generator runs with a bounty-submission format, it reads the override log and the session's tool and AI records, computes the intersection, and:

- If non-empty: refuses submission-ready output, names each override.
- --force-submit overrides the refusal, writes another log entry, marks the report with a warning banner.

### What the log is not

Not a legal instrument. Not a license. Not a substitute for judgment. It makes the override expensive, not impossible.

### Why mandatory

Because a silent override makes the policy advisory. Under pressure, advisory policies get ignored. A mandatory log prevents the unrecorded override. That is the difference between a compliance failure and a compliance failure with a paper trail, and that difference matters if a program ever asks how you found something.

## §26.2 — Core analysis capabilities

### §26.2.1 — Differential session analysis

Record two authenticated sessions (Principal A, B). Replay the same logical request from each. Diff responses. Emit findings only on violations.

Not fuzzing. Not brute-forcing. Not parameter tampering. Replays requests the hunter already made, with the identity changed.

Data model:

- SessionPair: a_session_id, b_session_id, target_host, program_slug, created_at
- RequestPair: a_request, b_request, logical_key, created_at
- DiffResult: request_pair_id, classification, evidence, severity

Classification values: cross_owner_read, cross_owner_write, unauthenticated_success, lower_privilege_success, precondition_violation, no_violation, inconclusive.

Classification rules (deterministic):

- A 200, B 403/401, same logical request, B is owner -> cross_owner_read
- A 200/201/204 on state-changing method, B cannot perform -> cross_owner_write or lower_privilege_success
- Unauthenticated request indistinguishable from authenticated -> unauthenticated_success
- State-requiring step succeeds without prior state -> precondition_violation
- A and B both 200 with identical bodies for per-principal request -> inconclusive

No finding without evidence pointing at both principals' requests.

Input: HAR (existing adapter), Burp XML (existing importer).

Commands:

    whaxon bounty pair  <session-a> <session-b>
    whaxon bounty diff  <pair-id>
    whaxon bounty report <pair-id>

Maps to: IDOR, horizontal privesc, vertical privesc, broken auth, broken function-level access control, business logic.
Status: DRAFT.

### §26.2.2 — Invariant engine

Declarative assumptions checked across sequential actions. Hunter writes invariants.yaml. Add-on executes the sequence and checks each invariant.

Not a fuzzer. Not a model checker.

Why it exists: "Business Logic Errors with real security impact" is on every qualifying list, and no scanner tests for it.

Schema:

session_principal: a

sequence:
  - id: login
    request: { method: POST, path: /auth/login, body: "user={{alias}}&pass={{password}}" }
    capture: { session_cookie: "Set-Cookie" }
  - id: create_invoice

    capture: { invoice_id: "$.id" }
  - id: refund
    request: { method: POST, path: "/invoices/{{invoice_id}}/refund" }

### Redaction boundary

Redaction is a report-generation concern, not an ingest concern. The store holds unredacted evidence: request and response headers verbatim, bodies truncated but not scrubbed. The submission-format generator (yeswehack, hackerone, bugcrowd) applies the program's redaction rules immediately before output, driven by reporting.redact_fields in the program policy. Internal formats (md, json) do not redact. This preserves the evidence the differential engine needs (§26.2.1) while ensuring nothing unredacted reaches a program's submission form.

## §26.8 — Legal and ethical boundary

Three structural constraints:

1. No request fires that a policy does not authorize. Either the target has no policy (existing WHAXON behavior) or the policy permits the action. The override log records every override.
2. No scope violation. Two gates: program scope and host scope. Both must pass. Out-of-scope wins.

The add-on does not decide what is legal. The operator decides, the policy records the decision, and the log records the override. The rest is the operator's responsibility.

# Business-impact severity — PR 3 design

Status: historical design record
Non-goals: this doc does not design the web UI's use of the field (a later UI session). The report-rendering changes were planned as a follow-on unit in the original design sequence; this statement is historical, not current status.

## 1. What this adds

A second severity scale on every finding, alongside technical severity.
Technical severity answers "how bad is the CVE" (CVSS, EPSS, KEV).
Business impact answers "what does this cost us" — the finding's kind,
the engagement context, and the incident-cost distributions the finding
class maps to.

The two scales are shown side-by-side. Neither collapses into the other.

## 2. Where it is computed

At resolve time, in `core/severity.py`, stored on the finding's
enrichment. Not at render time.

Reason: the web UI's Findings page needs the same value. Computing once
at resolve time and storing it means both surfaces read the same field,
the same way `severity_source` (PR 1a) does.

The field is `business_impact`. Vocabulary: `"critical"`, `"high"`,
`"medium"`, `"low"` — lowercase, same words as technical severity.

## 3. How the band is computed

Table-driven, in `core/business_impact.py::base_band`. Deterministic, no
LLM (R71).

Base band per finding kind, with three special cases:

- **correlated / vulnerable_service** — inherit the CVSS-derived
  severity from the severity resolver.
- **open_port** — `high` when the nmap adapter sets `lookup_hint`
  (indicating a versioned service), else `low`.
- **web_issue** — `high` for exploitability classes (SQLi, command
  injection, RCE, XSS, XXE, SSRF, LFI, RFI, matched on the finding's
  message and raw_line); else `low`.

Every other kind uses `_BASE_BY_KIND`. Unknown kinds default to `low`.

**Target modifier.** A finding against a production target shifts up
one band. Production is defined by `_is_production_target`:
hostnames with a real TLD, and IPs outside RFC1918/loopback and the lab
markers (`.test`, `.local`, `localhost`, `::1`, `scanme.nmap.org`).

## 4. Cost ranges

Each finding class carries a cost-range row: incident pattern, median
USD, p90 USD (or `None` when not published), source string.

Rows cite Cyentia IRIS 2025 (median and p90 by pattern), Verizon DBIR
2026 (median ransom payment), and — listed in `SOURCES_NOTE` but not
yet cited by any row — the Zenodo ICS Ransomware Dataset, ITIC 2025,
GDPR Article 83, and HIPAA Journal 2025.

**The mapping from WHAXON finding kind to incident pattern is WHAXON's
derivation, not the sources'.** This is stated in the module docstring
and in `SOURCES_NOTE`, and appears in the report.

## 5. Known gaps, deferred with rationale

- **`_COST_RANGES["web_app_breach"]` cites IRIS 2022 while every other
  row cites IRIS 2025.** Deferred until the 2025 row is confirmed;
  cross-source citing is defensible but should be uniform.
- **`_COST_RANGES["ransom_payment"]` names DBIR 2026 as the source but
  the Zenodo dataset is listed in `SOURCES_NOTE` and not cited on any
  row.** Either Zenodo-backed rows get added, or it comes out of the
  note.
- **`_BASE_BY_KIND` has `open_port_vulnerable` and
  `web_issue_exploitable` keys that no branch reaches** — the actual
  kinds are handled by dedicated branches. Dead rows.
- **`_annotate`'s `except Exception` swallows all failures** to `"low"`
  with no log. Same shape as the silent-swallow that hid the dead
  correlation rule in §21.13.
- **Report rendering of `business_impact` is not in this PR.** Edit A
  (rank top findings by business impact), Edit B (shift remediation
  bands by business impact), Edit C (print both scales in the appendix)
  are a follow-on unit.

## 6. Test plan

- Unit tests for `_is_production_target`: RFC1918, loopback, `.test`,

- Unit tests for `_shift_up`: already-critical stays critical;

- Integration test: `resolve_stored.resolve_stored` writes the field
